## Textbook Chapter 2.1–2.5 (Pages 30–57): Mesopotamia & Egypt

### Vocabulary
- **Mesopotamia** – "Land between the rivers" (Tigris and Euphrates); the region in present-day Iraq where some of the earliest complex societies developed.
- **Complex Society** – A society with features like cities, specialized labor, social hierarchy, organized religion, and a system of writing.
- **Hammurabi** – King of Babylon (c. 1792–1750 BCE) famous for his law code, one of the earliest written legal systems, based on the idea of proportional justice ("an eye for an eye").
- **City-State** – An independent political unit consisting of a city and its surrounding territory (e.g., Sumerian cities like Ur and Uruk).
- **Cuneiform** – The wedge-shaped writing system developed by the Sumerians, pressed into clay tablets; used for record-keeping, law, and literature.
- **Pharaoh** – The title of the ruler of ancient Egypt, considered a semi-divine, absolute monarch who unified religious and political authority.
- **Hieroglyphics** – The ancient Egyptian writing system using pictorial symbols, used for religious and monumental inscriptions.
- **Iron** – A metal that came into widespread use after 1000 BCE; stronger than bronze and more available, it changed warfare and agriculture.
- **Monotheism** – The belief in a single, all-powerful god, as practiced by the ancient Hebrews — a major departure from the polytheism common elsewhere in the region.
- **Jew** – Follower of Judaism, the monotheistic religion of the ancient Hebrews centered on a covenant with one God.

### Key Concept Questions
**How did the core civilizations in Mesopotamia and Egypt use laws, language, literature, religion, and monumental architecture to unify their regions?**
Mesopotamian city-states used cuneiform writing to record laws (like Hammurabi's Code), administer trade, and preserve religious/literary texts (such as the Epic of Gilgamesh), which created shared cultural identity across otherwise independent city-states. In Egypt, the pharaoh's semi-divine status fused political and religious authority, hieroglyphic writing recorded religious beliefs and royal achievements, and monumental architecture (pyramids, temples) reinforced the pharaoh's power and the unity of the Nile Valley under a single ruler.

**Describe Egypt's type of government.**
Egypt was ruled by a pharaoh, an absolute monarch believed to be a living god (or the earthly representative of the gods). This theocratic monarchy centralized political, religious, and economic power in one figure, supported by a bureaucracy of priests and officials.

**What were the features of the early systems of exchange in the eastern Mediterranean?**
Trade networks connected Egypt, Mesopotamia, and surrounding regions, moving goods like grain, timber, metals, and luxury items. Seafaring peoples such as the Phoenicians expanded maritime trade routes across the Mediterranean, spreading not just goods but also technologies like the alphabet.

**What was the main characteristic of monotheism among the ancient Hebrews?**
The ancient Hebrews believed in one exclusive, all-powerful God who had made a covenant (special agreement) with them, setting them apart from surrounding polytheistic cultures and establishing a moral/legal code (e.g., the Ten Commandments) tied to religious identity.

## Textbook Chapter 3.1–3.4 (Pages 62–85): South Asia

### Vocabulary
- **Ashoka** – Emperor of the Mauryan Dynasty who converted to Buddhism after the Kalinga War and used it to promote moral rule and unify his empire.
- **Dharma** – A core concept in Indian religions referring to duty, righteousness, and cosmic law/order.
- **Monsoon** – Seasonal winds that enabled predictable sea travel across the Indian Ocean, boosting long-distance trade.
- **Indus River Valley** – Site of an early complex society (Harappa, Mohenjo-Daro) in present-day Pakistan/northwest India, known for planned cities and standardized weights/measures.
- **Nomads** – People without a fixed settlement who move with herds or in search of resources; important in Central/South Asian history (e.g., Aryan migrations).
- **Mauryan Dynasty** – First empire to unify most of the Indian subcontinent (c. 321–185 BCE); reached its height under Ashoka.
- **Dhows** – Traditional sailing vessels used by Arab and Indian Ocean traders, well-suited to monsoon winds.

### Key Concept Questions
**What were the core beliefs of Buddha?**
The Buddha taught the Four Noble Truths (life involves suffering, suffering is caused by desire/attachment, suffering can end, and the Eightfold Path leads to that end) and the Eightfold Path as a guide to ethical and mental discipline leading toward enlightenment (nirvana), rejecting the rigid caste-based ritualism of orthodox Vedic religion.

**How did Ashoka attempt to use Buddhism to strengthen the Mauryan state?**
After witnessing the bloodshed of the Kalinga War, Ashoka converted to Buddhism and promoted it through edicts carved on pillars and rocks throughout the empire, emphasizing nonviolence, tolerance, and moral governance. This gave the diverse empire a shared ethical framework and cast Ashoka as a benevolent ruler, helping legitimize and unify Mauryan rule.

**Describe the natural features of the Indus River Valley Civilization (use the Travels of Ashoka map to describe).**
The Indus Valley civilization developed along the Indus River in a fertile floodplain bordered by mountains (Himalayas/Hindu Kush) to the north and desert to the west, which provided natural irrigation for agriculture but also somewhat isolated the region, while mountain passes still allowed contact with Central and West Asia.

**What were the features of Indian Ocean trade during the classical period?**
Trade relied on monsoon winds, which blew reliably in one direction for part of the year and reversed for the other part, allowing sailors using dhows to plan round trips. This network connected East Africa, Arabia, India, and Southeast Asia, exchanging goods like spices, textiles, and precious stones, along with religious and cultural ideas.

## Textbook Chapter 4.1–4.4 (Pages 90–113): East Asia

### Vocabulary
- **First emperor of the Qin dynasty** – Qin Shi Huang, who unified China in 221 BCE, standardized weights, measures, writing, and currency, and began construction connecting sections of the Great Wall.
- **Shang Dynasty** – Early Chinese dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE) known for bronze work, early writing (oracle bones), and a stratified society.
- **Oracle bone** – Animal bones or turtle shells used by Shang diviners to predict the future and communicate with ancestors/spirits; among the earliest evidence of Chinese writing.
- **Ancestor worship** – The practice of honoring and making offerings to deceased family members, central to Chinese religious and social life.
- **Mandate of Heaven** – The Zhou dynasty's political/religious concept that heaven grants rulers the right to govern, but withdraws it from unjust rulers (justifying dynastic change).
- **Confucius** – Chinese philosopher (551–479 BCE) whose teachings on ethics, hierarchy, and proper relationships formed the basis of Confucianism.
- **Confucianism** – A philosophy emphasizing social harmony through proper relationships, filial piety, respect for hierarchy, and moral leadership by example.
- **Daoism** – A philosophy founded by Laozi emphasizing living in harmony with the natural order (the Dao) and rejecting rigid social structures.
- **Legalism** – A political philosophy holding that human nature is selfish and that a strong state needs strict laws and harsh punishments to maintain order; adopted by the Qin.

### Key Concept Questions
**How did the Shang dynasty and Confucianism contribute to the development of Chinese culture during the classical age?**
The Shang dynasty established early Chinese writing, bronze metallurgy, and religious practices like ancestor worship and oracle bone divination, laying cultural foundations. Later, Confucianism built on these values by emphasizing filial piety, social hierarchy, and ethical governance, shaping Chinese family structure, education, and bureaucracy for centuries.

**How did the Qin dynasty build and administer its empire?**
The Qin unified China through military conquest and then centralized control by abolishing feudal states, standardizing writing, currency, weights and measures, and law under Legalist principles. The state also built infrastructure like roads and connected the Great Wall, using forced labor and strict, uniformly enforced laws to maintain control.

**What aspects of the Qin dynasty did the Han adopt, and what aspects did they modify?**
The Han kept the Qin's centralized bureaucracy, standardized administration, and unified infrastructure, but softened its harsh Legalist punishments, adopting Confucianism as the guiding philosophy of government and instituting a merit-based civil service exam system to recruit officials.

**How did the Han promote political unity, and how successful were they in maintaining it?**
The Han promoted unity through a Confucian-trained bureaucracy, centralized administration, standardized currency/writing carried over from the Qin, and state-sponsored Confucian education. This allowed the Han to maintain a long, relatively stable rule (over 400 years), though it eventually weakened due to court corruption, peasant uprisings, and regional warlordism.

## Textbook Chapter 6.1–6.4 (Pages 147–176): Persia, Greece & the Hellenistic World

### Vocabulary
- **Satrap** – A provincial governor in the Persian Empire, appointed to administer a satrapy (province) on behalf of the king.
- **Zoroastrianism** – A Persian religion founded by Zoroaster centered on a cosmic struggle between good and evil, with an emphasis on free will and moral choice.
- **Cyrus** – Cyrus the Great, founder of the Persian (Achaemenid) Empire, known for his policy of tolerance toward conquered peoples' religions and customs.
- **Darius I** – Persian king who expanded and organized the empire's administrative system, including satraps, a standardized code of laws, and the Royal Road.
- **Aristotle** – Greek philosopher who studied logic, natural science, ethics, and politics, and tutored Alexander the Great.
- **Alexander of Macedon** – Alexander the Great, who conquered the Persian Empire and extended Greek/Macedonian rule from Greece to Egypt and into India, spreading Hellenistic culture.
- **Hellenism** – The blending of Greek culture with the cultures of the regions Alexander conquered, spreading Greek language, art, and ideas across the Mediterranean and Near East.

### Key Concept Questions
**What techniques of imperial administration did the Persians develop in order to construct and administer their empire?**
The Persians divided their empire into satrapies governed by satraps who collected tribute and administered local affairs, while royal inspectors ("the King's Eyes and Ears") monitored their loyalty. They built the Royal Road and a relay messenger system to speed communication, used a common language (Aramaic) for administration, and practiced religious/cultural tolerance to keep conquered peoples cooperative.

**What were the core ideas in Greco-Roman philosophy and science, and how were those ideas put into practical application?**
Greek philosophers like Aristotle emphasized observation, logic, and reason to understand the natural world and human ethics/politics. These ideas were applied practically in fields like medicine, engineering, and governance, and Hellenistic centers like Alexandria became hubs of scientific research in astronomy, geometry, and anatomy.

**How did Alexander's empire serve as a basis for interregional trade, communication, and exchange?**
Alexander's conquests connected Greece, Egypt, Persia, and parts of India under one political sphere, and the Greek cities he founded (like Alexandria) became hubs of trade and cultural exchange. Greek became a common administrative and commercial language, facilitating the spread of goods, ideas, and Hellenistic culture across the region even after his empire fragmented.

**What aspect of Persian political forms did the Parthians and Sasanians adapt?**
The Parthians and Sasanians, who ruled Persia after Alexander's empire fragmented, adopted the earlier Achaemenid model of a centralized monarchy supported by provincial governors, along with Persian traditions of religious legitimacy (including Zoroastrianism) to justify royal authority.

## Textbook Chapter 7.1–7.4 (Pages 181–208): Rome

### Vocabulary
- **Roman republic** – The system of government in Rome (c. 509–27 BCE) featuring elected officials, a senate, and checks on individual power, ended by the rise of emperors.
- **Roman senate** – A council of aristocrats (patricians) that advised magistrates and held significant influence over Roman law, finance, and foreign policy.
- **Carthage** – A powerful Phoenician city-state in North Africa and Rome's chief rival in the Mediterranean, defeated in the Punic Wars.
- **Punic Wars** – A series of three wars (264–146 BCE) between Rome and Carthage that ended with Rome's total victory and destruction of Carthage, securing Roman dominance in the western Mediterranean.
- **Dictator** – In the Roman Republic, a temporary office granting one leader absolute emergency power for a limited term (e.g., six months).
- **Julius Caesar** – Roman general and statesman whose rise to power and assassination (44 BCE) marked the collapse of the Republic and the transition toward imperial rule.
- **Jesus** – Jewish preacher whose teachings and crucifixion under Roman rule became the foundation of Christianity.
- **Constantine** – Roman emperor who legalized Christianity (Edict of Milan, 313 CE) and later moved the capital to Constantinople, reshaping the empire's religious and political future.

### Key Concept Questions
**What cultural characteristics did Rome possess that allowed it to administer such a large empire?**
Rome extended citizenship and legal rights to conquered peoples, built an extensive network of roads and aqueducts for communication and trade, adopted a shared legal system (Roman law), and tolerated local religions and customs as long as subjects acknowledged Roman authority — all of which helped integrate a diverse, far-flung empire.

**What administrative and social institutions did Rome create in order to govern its empire, and how did those institutions change with the transition from republican Rome to imperial Rome?**
Under the Republic, elected magistrates, the Senate, and a system of checks and balances governed Rome, with power shared among aristocrats. As the Republic gave way to the Empire (starting with Augustus), power concentrated in the emperor, the Senate became largely symbolic, and a professional bureaucracy and standing army administered the vast territory directly under imperial authority.

**What were the core beliefs of Christianity, and why were those beliefs so appealing to people in the Roman Empire?**
Christianity taught monotheism, salvation through faith in Jesus Christ, and the promise of an afterlife, along with messages of equality and compassion regardless of social status. These ideas appealed strongly to the poor, slaves, and women, who found less status distinction in Christian communities than in Roman society, and the promise of eternal salvation offered hope amid the hardships of imperial life.

**What were the political, cultural, and administrative reasons for the collapse of the Western Roman Empire?**
Politically, the empire suffered from succession crises, civil wars, and weak/short-lived emperors; administratively, the empire became too large and costly to defend and govern effectively, leading to overtaxation and economic strain; culturally, the empire faced pressure from migrating and invading peoples (such as the Goths and Huns) that Rome could no longer fully assimilate or repel, ultimately leading to the fall of the Western Empire in 476 CE (while the Eastern/Byzantine Empire continued).
